[P0000 | Normal] Sheep (Ovis aries L.) constitute one of humanity's most genetically diverse livestock species, with more than 1,300 recognised breeds worldwide, each representing unique adaptations to extreme environmental conditions, including high-altitude hypoxia, arid-zone heat stress, disease endemicity, and nutritional marginality (Dossybayev et al., 2025; Hansen et al., 2025; Wanjala et al., 2025) This genetic diversity underpins global food security, ecosystem resilience, and the capacity of pastoral systems to adapt to accelerating climate change (Ma et al., 2025; Muthusamy et al., 2025). However, this biological heritage faces an unprecedented threat within the broad field of animal genetic resources: approximately 27% of sheep breeds are currently classified as endangered, with demographic bottlenecks, uncontrolled crossbreeding, and intensifying environmental pressures accelerating genetic erosion (Barłowska et al., 2025; Motaung et al., 2024; Tampaki et al., 2025). Narrowing to the specific context of conservation strategy, the Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) promotes integrated in situ and ex situ strategies to safeguard biodiversity (Engdawork et al., 2024). Among ex situ approaches, germplasm cryobanking, particularly sperm cryopreservation, provides a cost-effective, long-term solution (Castro et al., 2025; Shayestehyekta et al., 2026). The critical gap emerges here: conventional cryobanking relies primarily on elective semen collection from live animals. This approach cannot address the acute genetic emergencies posed by sudden mortality in valuable individuals. Thus, the specific problem is defined: irreversible genetic loss occurs when valuable rams die unexpectedly because post-slaughter recovery protocols lack optimised antioxidant protection against oxidative damage.
[P0001 | Normal] This study operates within a Positivist research paradigm, assuming an objective reality where oxidative stress markers and sperm viability parameters can be measured independently of the researcher to establish universal preservation protocols. Within this framework, consider the concrete scenario that motivates this investigation: a genetically elite Ouled Djellal ram, bearing polymorphisms in genes associated with enhanced meat production qualities and adaptation capability, dies abruptly of severe pneumonia at a research station in Djelfa, Algeria. The animal represents years of selective breeding for improved growth rates, carcass quality, and resilience in semi-arid to arid steppe environments, all features increasingly valued under climate change (Sebkhi et al., 2024). With live collection impossible, the question arises: what genetic material can be salvaged? Within 24-48 hours, if the carcass has been cooled appropriately, the cauda epididymides may yield 5–15 billion spermatozoa capable of fertilisation via assisted reproduction (Anciuti et al., 2025; Mujitaba et al., 2024). Without intervention, this genetic line is permanently lost; with optimised post-slaughter recovery and preservation, the ram's genetic contribution may extend across decades and geographies. Post-slaughter sperm recovery constitutes an increasingly critical strategy for genetic conservation (Engdawork et al., 2024; Silva et al., 2025). Unlike elective germplasm collection through electroejaculation, post-slaughter recovery captures genetic material that would otherwise be irretrievably lost. The technique has demonstrated practical success: pregnancy rates of 87.5% have been achieved with cryopreserved epididymal sperm from Hungarian Black Racka and Merino rams (Mujitaba et al., 2024; Sharafi et al., 2022). The epididymis functions as a natural reservoir of mature spermatozoa, recoverable within 24-48 hours post-mortem with minimal quality deterioration when testes are stored at 4-5°C (Shayestehyekta et al., 2022; Silva et al., 2025).
[P0002 | Normal] However, the efficacy of genetic rescue hinges on a narrow temporal window and the quality of preservation achieved within that window. The interval between death and sperm collection, combined with thermal conditions of carcass storage, constitutes the primary determinant of cellular integrity and functional viability (Shi et al., 2025). Upon somatic death, the cessation of systemic metabolic support initiates a rapid biochemical cascade: ischemia forces anaerobic glycolysis and ATP depletion; mitochondrial electron transport chain disruption generates reactive oxygen species (ROS); iron-catalysed Fenton chemistry produces hydroxyl radicals; and lipid peroxidation propagates membrane damage (Fleming & Thomson, 2025; Shi et al., 2025). This "golden hour" framework creates both opportunity and imperative for intervention (Rosato et al., 2021). Post-slaughter epididymal sperm constitute a biologically distinct preservation challenge, fundamentally different from conventionally processed ejaculated semen. Three specificities define this distinctiveness. First is the absence of seminal plasma protection; epididymal spermatozoa lack exposure to accessory gland secretions that provide antioxidant buffering (Van Tilburg et al., 2021). Second is ovine-specific structural vulnerability; ram spermatozoa possess the highest docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) content among domestic livestock, comprising 30–40% of plasma membrane phospholipid fatty acids, creating exceptional susceptibility to lipid peroxidation (Abdollahzadeh et al., 2025; Carro et al., 2022). Third is pre-existing oxidative damage; post-slaughter sperm enter preservation already compromised by post-mortem ROS exposure (Castro et al., 2025). This study addresses an Empirical Gap (no prior testing of Date Palm Pollen in ovine post-slaughter contexts) and a Methodological Gap (failure of single-mechanism antioxidants to address multi-source ROS generation).
[P0003 | Normal] Given established oxidative vulnerability, antioxidant supplementation would seem an obvious solution. However, critical analysis reveals fundamental limitations when conventional approaches are applied to post-slaughter contexts. Chemical antioxidants such as Trolox and cysteine operate through single mechanisms that cannot address the multi-source ROS generation characteristic of post-mortem deterioration (Kafi et al., 2024). Trolox's aqueous solubility restricts membrane integration; cysteine requires metabolic activation, compromised by post-mortem decline. Enzymatic antioxidants are membrane-impermeable and impractical for field application (Kurochkin & Pleshanov, 2024). This systematic failure creates a specific research imperative: the identification of antioxidant interventions capable of multi-target action, membrane integration, and sustainable field implementation. Date palm pollen (Phoenix dactylifera L.) emerges as uniquely positioned to address these requirements. DPP is a complex matrix of bioactive molecules, polyphenols, fatty acids, minerals, vitamins, and amino acids, acting through complementary mechanisms (Salhi et al., 2024). Polyphenol diversity enables multi-target ROS scavenging; amphiphilic character enables membrane integration; mitochondrial targeting preserves energetic function; and Nrf2 pathway activation provides sustained defence (Fallahi et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2019). Cross-species evidence supports DPP's reproductive benefits in bulls, goats, buffalo, and rabbits. However, no studies have evaluated DPP specifically in ovine sperm or in post-slaughter epididymal contexts in any species.
[P0004 | Normal] To ensure methodological precision, key variables are operationally defined: "Optimal Concentration" is defined as the DPP percentage yielding the highest progressive motility score post-thaw; "Functional Conservation" is defined as blastocyst development rate ≥ 30% in IVF assays; and "Post-Slaughter Window" is delimited to sperm recovered within 24 hours of death with carcass storage at 4°C. This investigation tests the hypothesis that date palm pollen supplementation to semen extender significantly improves the conservation outcomes of ovine epididymal sperm recovered post-slaughter. Four specific objectives guide the research. Objective 1: Establish optimal DPP concentration and timing for ovine post-slaughter epididymal sperm preservation. Objective 2: Compare DPP efficacy to conventional antioxidant (Trolox) supplementation. Objective 3: Elucidate mechanism-specific protection through assessment of mitochondrial ROS, lipid peroxidation, DNA oxidation, and antioxidant enzyme activity. Objective 4: Validate functional conservation through in vitro fertilization and embryo development to the blastocyst stage. Aligned with these objectives, four Alternative Hypotheses (Ha) and corresponding Null Hypotheses (H0) are proposed:
[P0005 | Normal] Ha1: DPP improves post-thaw quality in a dose-dependent manner, with an optimal concentration between 2–10%. H01: DPP supplementation has no significant effect on post-thaw quality across concentrations of 2–10%. Ha2: The conservative effect of DPP is mediated by specific mechanisms exceeding conventional antioxidant efficacy. H02: There is no significant difference in oxidative stress mechanisms between DPP and conventional antioxidant treatments. Ha3: Equilibration-period DPP addition outperforms post-thaw addition due to cellular integration. H03: Timing of DPP addition (equilibration vs. post-thaw) has no significant effect on sperm quality. Ha4: DPP-preserved sperm demonstrate improved fertilizing capacity in IVF with higher blastocyst rates than control. H04: There is no significant difference in blastocyst rates between DPP-preserved sperm and control groups.
[P0006 | Normal] This study is subject to specific delimitations: it is delimited to Ouled Djellal rams, excludes in vivo fertility trials, and is restricted to a 24-hour post-mortem window to ensure standardisation of post-mortem interval effects. This investigation represents the first systematic study of date palm pollen's conserved effects specifically in post-slaughter ovine epididymal sperm, addressing a critical translational gap. The significance of the study is articulated through two lenses: Theoretical Significance includes the establishment of species-specific DPP efficacy data for ovine sperm, mechanistic validation of polypharmacological antioxidant action in post-mortem cells, and comparative benchmarking against conventional antioxidants. Practical Significance involves a validated, field-applicable protocol for DPP supplementation in ovine genetic banking, quality control parameters for standardisation, and enhanced genetic salvage rates from post-slaughter recovery, reducing cost and infrastructure requirements for genetic banking in developing regions. The ultimate measure of success is practical: whether DPP enables effective genetic conservation from animals that would otherwise represent permanent genetic loss, bridging the critical gap between mortality in the field and perpetuation of valuable genotypes in breeding programs and gene banks.
