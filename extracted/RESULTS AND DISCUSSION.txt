[P0000 | Normal] RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
[P0001 | Normal] 4.1 Experiment 1: Socioeconomic Context and Trait Preferences of Sheep Keepers in El Oued, Algeria
[P0002 | Normal] 4.1.1 Overview and Rationale
[P0003 | Normal] Before evaluating the technical efficacy of date palm pollen (DPP) for post-slaughter sperm preservation, it is essential to establish the socioeconomic and conservation context within which such interventions would be implemented. This survey of 200 sheep keepers across eight municipalities in El Oued region, Southwest Algeria, provides critical baseline data on: (a) the demographic profile of stakeholders in ovine genetic conservation; (b) flock characteristics and management systems; and (c) trait preferences that define breeding objectives and, consequently, the value of genetic rescue from post-slaughter recovery. These findings ground the technical research in field-level realities, ensuring that DPP-based preservation addresses genuine conservation priorities.
[P0004 | Normal] El Oued region represents an ideal study context: it is a major centre of both date palm cultivation and sheep production, creating geographic convergence between DPP availability and the genetic conservation need (Bouguedoura et al., 2015; Merrouchi & Sais, 2025a; Mihi & Hernández-Agüero, 2025) The region's arid climate, extensive pastoral systems, and reliance on indigenous sheep breeds adapted to harsh conditions exemplify contexts where post-slaughter genetic rescue is most valuable, where valuable genotypes are at risk of unrecorded loss due to limited cryobanking infrastructure (Laouadi et al., 2018a; Meziane et al., 2024; Ouchene-Khelifi et al., 2021).
[P0005 | Normal] 4.1.2 Data Cleaning and Pre-processing
[P0006 | Normal] 4.1.2.1 Data Pre-processing
[P0007 | Normal] Before analysis, the dataset underwent a cleaning protocol to ensure integrity, adhering to the "Janitor Phase" of research (Masterclass, Page 26). Incomplete responses were excluded, and categorical variables (e.g., occupation, education) were coded for statistical compatibility. No significant outliers were detected in the demographic variables, and the final sample consisted of 200 valid responses from registered sheep keepers across eight municipalities. A sensitivity audit confirmed that data cleaning did not alter the directional trends of the findings.
[P0008 | Normal] 4.1.3 Socioeconomic Characteristics of Surveyed Sheep Keepers
[P0009 | Normal] 4.1.3.1 Gender and Demographic Structure The demographic analysis revealed an exclusively male respondent pool (100%), reflecting the gendered nature of livestock ownership and decision-making in Algerian pastoral systems. This finding aligns with regional studies indicating male dominance in formal livestock management, such as Laouadi et al. (2018), who reported 94.3% male participation in the Laghouat area (citing earlier Laoubi et al., 2011 data from the same area), and Ouchene-Khelifi et al. (2021), who found 99.29% male goat producers in Algeria (noting similar patterns in Kabylia and other regions at 86.2–100% (Kadi et al., 2014). Recent work by Meziane et al. (2024) documented 100% male respondents in Batna sheep farming surveys. The complete absence of female respondents indicates structural barriers to women's participation in formal livestock management, despite their likely contributions to daily husbandry tasks (FAO, 2011; Laouadi et al., 2018b)
[P0010 | Normal] The age distribution indicated a maturing keeper population with limited youth engagement. Only 11% of respondents were younger than 25 years, while 29% were 36–45 years, and 21.5% were over 55 years. This ageing demographic structure poses significant challenges for long-term conservation sustainability. Similar trends have been documented across North Africa and the Sahel: Meziane et al. (2024) found comparable youth disinterest in Batna, Algeria; Amare et al. (2025) reported generational exit from pastoralism in Ethiopia; and Yakubu et al. (2020) noted ageing livestock keeper populations in Nigeria. The low attractiveness of sheep farming to young people, attributed to perceived low profitability, physical demands, and preference for urban employment, threatens the intergenerational transmission of indigenous breeding knowledge (Blench, 2001; Nori, 2022)

[P0012 | Normal] 4.1.3.2 Education and Human Capital
[P0013 | Normal] Educational attainment was moderate but limited, with 38.5% completing only primary schooling or less and 23.5% completing middle school. Only 11.5% had a university education. This profile is comparable to recent Algerian livestock sector studies (Meziane et al., 2024) but represents an improvement over earlier decades; Boussaada and Yerou (2013) reported 60% illiteracy in M'sila's semi-arid region, a result similar to 56%, confirmed by Saad (2024) in the region. The 38.5% with limited formal education has critical implications for technology adoption: DPP-based preservation must be technically simple, visually demonstrable, and require minimal literacy-dependent protocols (Gizaw et al., 2010; Guresci et al., 2024)
[P0014 | Normal] Education level significantly influences trait preferences and technology adoption in livestock systems (Oli et al., 2025). Higher education correlates with greater willingness to invest in genetic improvement, record-keeping, and novel reproductive technologies (Van Dijke et al., 2022). However, the relatively low literacy level in El-Oued can be attributed to the remoteness of schools and limited access to educational infrastructure, especially for people who live in rural area (Baker & Gray, 2004; Richmond et al., 2008). Educated farmers are more likely to prioritise traits that enhance productivity and resilience, while less educated farmers may rely on traditional knowledge and prioritise visible traits like body size or coat colour (Gizaw et al, 2013; Thobile et al., 2021).
[P0015 | Normal] 4.1.3.3 Occupation and Livelihood Dependence
[P0016 | Normal] Sheep breeding was the primary occupation for 71% of respondents, with 14% engaged in free occupations/trading and 6.5% in civil employment. This high dependence on livestock indicates a strong incentive for genetic investment and conservation. Keepers are professionally committed to flock productivity rather than maintaining animals as a supplementary activity (Amare et al., 2025; Siad et al., 2022; Yakubu et al., 2020; Youcefi & Marouf, 2024). The 28% with >20 years' experience and 22% with 10–15 years' experience represent substantial accumulated practical knowledge that could support the adaptive implementation of DPP protocols (Faradji et al., 2022; Meziane et al., 2024). Experience plays a critical role in shaping trait preferences; experienced farmers are more likely to prioritise traits that ensure long-term flock productivity and resilience, such as drought tolerance and health status (Amare et al., 2025; Yousuf et al., 2025).
[P0017 | Normal] However, the 16% with <5 years' experience represents a vulnerable group: novice keepers may lack technical skills for optimal genetic management and be most at risk of losing valuable genetics through unrecorded mortality (Mennani et al., 2025; Meziane et al., 2024; Tezera & Engidashet, 2022). Targeted extension supporting post-slaughter recovery protocols for this demographic could yield significant conservation returns.
[P0018 | Normal] 4.1.4 Farm and Flock Characteristics
[P0019 | Normal] 4.1.4.1 Geographic Distribution
[P0020 | Normal] Respondents were distributed across eight municipalities, with concentration in Robbah (28.5%) and Taleb Larbi (24.5%), together comprising 53% of the sample. This clustering reflects the historical development of sheep farming in these areas, likely driven by access to grazing resources, market infrastructure, and social networks supporting pastoral livelihoods (Bouguedoura et al., 2015) . The geographic spread ensures representation of microclimatic and management variation across El Oued's pastoral zone.

[P0022 | Normal] 4.1.4.2 Flock Size Structure
[P0023 | Normal] Flock size distribution revealed predominance of small-to-medium operations: 38.5% maintained <20 sheep, 41% had 20–50 sheep, and only 6.5% exceeded 100 head. This structure is characteristic of North African pastoral systems (Laoun et al., 2026; Meziane et al., 2024; Ouali et al., 2023) and has critical implications for genetic conservation:
[P0024 | Normal] Small flock vulnerability: Keepers with <20 animals have limited genetic diversity and high extinction risk from single mortality events—precisely the context where post-slaughter recovery is most valuable (Gaouar et al., 2017; Giovannini et al., 2024).
[P0025 | Normal] Medium flock potential: The 41% with 20–50 sheep represent an optimal scale for DPP protocol adoption, sufficient genetic value to justify preservation effort, yet manageable without extensive infrastructure (Moula & Djebala, 2025; Siad et al., 2022).
[P0026 | Normal] Limited commercial sector: The small proportion with >100 sheep indicates the absence of intensive breeding operations that might maintain private cryobanks; genetic conservation relies heavily on individual keeper decisions and accessible, low-cost preservation tools (Abdelkader et al., 2018; Yousuf et al., 2025).
[P0027 | Normal] 4.1.4.3 Herd Acquisition and Genetic Management
[P0028 | Normal] The 73% who acquired herds through both inheritance and market purchase practised strategic genetic mixing, combining family lineages with introduced genetics. This approach balances tradition (maintaining locally adapted genotypes) with innovation (accessing improved traits), but creates genetic recording challenges: animals of mixed origin may have an undocumented pedigree, and valuable genotypes may be lost when purchased breeding males die unexpectedly (Gaouar et al., 2015, 2017; Yakubu et al., 2020).
[P0029 | Normal] The 10.5% relying solely on inheritance maintain potentially unique, long-selected lineages that are particularly vulnerable to genetic erosion—post-slaughter recovery from these flocks could capture rare alleles absent from commercial populations (Meziane et al., 2024; Ouali et al., 2023).
[P0030 | Normal] 4.1.5 Trait Preferences: Defining Genetic Conservation Priorities
[P0031 | Normal] Trait preferences reveal breeding objectives that determine the value of genetic rescue. Keepers prioritise traits that would be lost without effective post-slaughter preservation, establishing the conservation imperative that DPP-based protocols must address.
[P0032 | Normal] 4.1.5.1 Male Selection Criteria
[P0033 | Normal] Breed was the most highly preferred trait (27% first preference), followed by good body conformation (12.5%), apparent health status (12%), and big body size (11%). This ranking reflects functional breeding objectives adapted to arid environments:
[P0034 | Normal] Breed priority: Indicates awareness of genetic adaptation, indigenous breeds (likely Ouled Djellal or similar) are valued for drought tolerance, disease resistance, and production under nutritional stress (Gaouar et al., 2015; Mennani et al., 2025). The emphasis on breed validates genetic conservation as a keeper priority, supporting investment in post-slaughter recovery.
[P0035 | Normal] Body conformation and size: Reflect market and breeding value, well-conformed, large rams command premium prices and sire desirable offspring (Belhaj et al., 2021; Mohammedi, 2023). Loss of such animals represents a high economic and genetic cost.
[P0036 | Normal] Health status (12%) and disease resistance (3.5%): The gap between these rankings suggests keepers prioritise observable health over genetic resistance, potentially undervaluing disease resistance that could be captured through post-slaughter genetic rescue (Moulla et al., 2018; Taherti et al., 2023).
[P0037 | Normal] Reproductive traits, big testicles (6%), virility/vigour (6%), were moderately prioritised, indicating awareness of male fertility importance. This directly supports the thesis focus: preserving sperm from genetically valuable, fertile males addresses a recognised keeper priority.

[P0041 | Normal] Big udder size (10%) indicates milk production importance for household nutrition and lamb rearing, while good body conformation (9%) and docility (7%) reflect management and market considerations (Harkat et al., 2024; Yabrir et al., 2013)
[P0042 | Normal] The lower emphasis on rapid growth (4%) and young age (2.5%) in females versus males suggests long-term productivity prioritisation over early performance, consistent with maternal selection objectives.

[P0047 | Normal] 4.1.6 Statistical Analysis: Determinants of Trait Preferences
[P0048 | Normal] Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted to examine the effects of socioeconomic variables on trait preferences. Pillai's Trace was selected as the test statistic due to its robustness against assumption violations (Olson, 1976; Tabachnick & Fidell, 2025). The analysis revealed a significant three-way interaction between education, experience, and occupation. No other main effects or interactions reached significance. This synergistic effect indicates that human capital operates as a complex system rather than additive components; highly educated, experienced, full-time breeders prioritise fundamentally different traits than less educated, inexperienced, part-time keepers (Abebe et al., 2020; Duguma et al., 2011; Gizaw et al., 2018).

[P0051 | Normal] 4.1.7 Discussion: Implications for Post-Slaughter Genetic Rescue and DPP Application
[P0052 | Normal] 4.1.7.1 Conservation Context: Where DPP-Based Preservation Fits
[P0053 | Normal] The survey findings establish the critical contextual factors for the technical research. The prioritisation of breed and adaptation traits confirms that keepers manage genetically valuable, locally evolved flocks vulnerable to unrecorded mortality. Indigenous breeds like Ouled Djellal are prized for drought tolerance, hardiness under nutritional stress, and resilience in arid steppe/Saharan conditions (Gaouar et al., 2015; Mennani et al., 2025). The predominance of small flocks and absence of intensive commercial operations indicate limited private cryobanking capacity, creating a niche for accessible, low-cost, field-implementable tools like DPP-based preservation (Feugang et al., 2026; Giovannini et al., 2024).
[P0054 | Normal] Furthermore, the co-location of date palm cultivation and sheep production in El Oued creates a unique opportunity for DPP utilisation. Keepers already value both components of this agroecological system—oasis date palm groves provide microclimates, fodder integration, and polyculture benefits for livestock, while sheep farming complements subsistence in Saharan family systems (Ariom et al., 2022; Merrouchi & Sais, 2025b). DPP-based sperm preservation represents value-added utilisation of a familiar, locally available resource rather than the introduction of exotic technology. The high dependence on breeding as a primary occupation suggests a receptive audience for genetic conservation extension if practical tools are provided.
[P0055 | Normal] 4.1.7.2 Socioeconomic Barriers and Technology Transfer
[P0056 | Normal] The demographic structure highlights specific barriers to technology adoption. The ageing keeper population and limited youth engagement threaten the intergenerational transmission of indigenous breeding knowledge (Nori, 2022; Sharifian et al., 2022). Additionally, the moderate educational attainment levels demand simple, robust protocols without complex equipment or literacy-dependent procedures. DPP aqueous extraction meets this requirement, aligning with the need for visually demonstrable techniques (Bontsa et al., 2023; Smidt & Jokonya, 2022).
[P0057 | Normal] The significant interaction between education, experience, and occupation suggests that extension strategies must be targeted. Experienced professionals may adopt sophisticated preservation for elite genetics, while simplified protocols for novice keepers could prevent routine genetic loss. This aligns with literature suggesting that human capital factors operate synergistically in shaping breeding decisions (Abebe et al., 2020; Duguma et al., 2011).
[P0058 | Normal] 4.1.7.3 Alignment with Thesis Objectives
[P0059 | Normal] The trait preferences define the valuable genotypes for rescue. The emphasis on drought tolerance, particularly for females, underscores the climate adaptation value of local genotypes that DPP-based preservation could protect. Indigenous Algerian breeds like Ouled Djellal are prized for maternal hardiness, drought and high-temperature resistance, good reproductive qualities, and production under nutritional stress, traits expressed strongly in ewes for lamb survival and protection in harsh arid conditions (Gaouar et al., 2017; Mennani et al., 2025; Sebkhi et al., 2024). The technical experiments evaluate preservation of precisely these characteristics, ensuring that the biological outcomes match the socioeconomic priorities.
[P0060 | Normal] The universal priority on breed/genetic background across studies validates the conservation focus of this thesis: keepers intrinsically value genetic resources and will likely support preservation technologies that maintain breed integrity (Duguma et al., 2011; Youcefi & Marouf, 2024).
[P0061 | Normal] 4.1.7.4 Limitations and Future Research Directions
[P0062 | Normal] While this survey provides a cross-sectional snapshot, longitudinal tracking would reveal the temporal dynamics of sheep keeper demographics and preferences. The absence of female respondents limits understanding of gendered knowledge and potential women's roles in DPP protocol implementation, future research should explicitly engage women in livestock management. Quantitative estimation of genetic loss from mortality would strengthen the economic case for post-slaughter recovery investment. Future research should also focus on genomic characterization of local breeds and the development of tailored breeding strategies to ensure the long-term sustainability of sheep farming in El-Oued and comparable dryland regions.
[P0063 | Normal] 4.1.7 Conclusion
[P0064 | Normal] Experiment 1 establishes that El Oued's sheep keepers manage genetically valuable, climate-adapted flocks using traditional systems vulnerable to genetic erosion. The convergence of date palm availability, keeper prioritization of genetic quality, and limited cryobanking infrastructure creates optimal conditions for DPP-based preservation. The full-time breeders with substantial experience represent ready adopters of practical genetic rescue tools, while small flock sizes and education constraints demand technically simple, field-applicable solutions. These findings validate the conservation imperative and implementation context for the technical experiments that follow.
[P0065 | Normal] 4.2 Experiment 2: Proximate Composition, Mineral Profile, and Bioactive Properties of Date Palm Pollen (DPP)
[P0066 | Normal] 4.2.1 Overview and Rationale
[P0067 | Normal] Following the establishment of the socioeconomic context in Experiment 1, Experiment 2 characterises the nutritional and phytochemical profile of Date Palm Pollen (DPP) sourced from the El Oued region. This analysis provides the mechanistic baseline required to evaluate DPP as a cryoprotective agent in Experiment 3. The El Oued region represents a geographic convergence of date palm cultivation and sheep production, making local DPP characterisation particularly relevant for subsequent application in ovine genetic conservation (Bouguedoura et al., 2015; Selmani et al., 2017a). The arid climate and specific cultivar types of this region may yield distinct phytochemical profiles compared to DPP sourced from other agroecological zones, including higher phenolics, flavonoids, and antioxidants adapted to Saharan stress (Benamor et al., 2024; Salhi et al., 2024). Understanding these regional specificities ensures that the DPP-based preservation protocols developed in Experiment 3 utilise materials with documented compositional properties.
[P0068 | Normal] Furthermore, the nutritional density and antioxidant capacity of DPP have direct implications for its protective potential. High protein content may stabilise cellular membranes, while antioxidant compounds could mitigate oxidative stress during sperm storage, mechanisms well-documented in conventional preservation media (Ashour et al., 2023; El-Kholy et al., 2019; Laghouati et al., 2021). This compositional analysis thus provides the foundational data for interpreting sperm preservation outcomes in subsequent experiments.
[P0069 | Normal] 4.2.2 Data Cleaning and Analytical Protocol
[P0070 | Normal] 4.2.2.1 Quality Control and Calibration Before compositional analysis, all instrumentation underwent calibration to ensure data integrity, adhering to the "Janitor Phase" of research (Masterclass, Page 26). Standard calibration curves were generated for polyphenol and flavonoid quantification, achieving high linearity (R² ≥ 0.96). All proximate and mineral analyses were conducted in triplicate (n=3) to minimise technical error. Outliers were identified using Z-scores; no extreme values were detected, and mean values with Standard Error of Mean (SEM) are reported. This rigorous pre-processing ensures that the compositional data accurately reflects the biological material rather than analytical artefacts.
[P0071 | Normal] 4.2.3 Proximate Composition of Date Palm Pollen
[P0072 | Normal] The proximate analysis revealed substantial nutritional value in El Oued-sourced DPP, with composition varying across parameters in ways that both align with and diverge from previous literature (Table 4.6). These discrepancies underscore the influence of cultivar type, agroecological conditions, harvest timing, and post-harvest processing on DPP quality, factors critical for standardising preservation protocols.
[P0073 | Normal] 4.2.3.1 Moisture Content
[P0074 | Normal] The moisture content determined in the present study (5.82 ± 0.15%) aligns closely with findings reported by Al-Samarai et al. (2016) (8.041 ± 0.1%) and other dried Algerian/regional samples, Laghouati et al. (2023) (7.34%), reflecting effective post-harvest drying and storage practices common in arid oasis systems. However, this value is substantially lower than those documented by Hassan (2011) (28.80%), Al-Anber (2017) (~39–44% range in Iraqi samples), and reviews compiling higher moisture in fresh/undried pollen (e.g., ~21–30% in Egyptian/Iraqi cultivars) (Salhi et al., 2024). The lower moisture content observed indicates effective post-harvest drying and storage practices, which are essential for preventing microbial contamination and maintaining phytochemical stability. Elevated moisture levels in earlier studies likely reflect the use of fresh or insufficiently dried pollen samples, which may have compromised long-term storage potential and antioxidant activity (Sebii et al., 2019). For preservation applications, reduced moisture content is advantageous as it minimises ice crystal formation and osmotic stress during freezing, critical factors in viability maintenance.
[P0075 | Normal] 4.2.3.2 Ash Content
[P0076 | Normal] The ash content of the current sample (6.10 ± 0.20%) falls within the broad range reported in literature (4.57% to 10.23%), consistent with values from Al-Samarai et al. (2016) (5.585 ± 0.144%), Jasim & Al-Anber (2017) (5.19%), and Hassan (2011) (4.57%). This intermediate ash level indicates moderate mineral concentration, suggesting DPP may contribute essential electrolytes to preservation media without excessive mineral load that could disrupt osmotic balance. Elevated ash levels typically correlate with higher mineral bioavailability, potentially enhancing DPP's value as a mineral source in extenders (El-Kholy et al., 2019). The variability across studies, from Hassan's (2011) 4.57% to higher values in some fresh/undried samples, likely reflects soil mineral composition differences, geographic origin, and cultivar-specific mineral accumulation patterns (Salhi et al., 2024).

[P0078 | Normal] 4.2.3.3 Crude Fat Content
[P0079 | Normal] The crude fat content (6.49 ± 0.18%) was marginally lower than values reported by Al-Samarai et al. (2016) (7.678 ± 0.149%), El-Kholy et al. (2019) (10.80 ± 0.03%), and Al-Anber (2017) (9.19%), but substantially below the higher levels documented by Hassan (2011) (20.74%) and ranges up to 31.50% in some cultivars (Salhi et al., 2024). Given that lipids serve as primary energy sources and membrane components, this moderate fat content suggests DPP could provide metabolic substrate during storage without excessive lipid oxidation risks. The lipid composition of DPP is influenced by cultivar genetics, environmental conditions (e.g., arid oasis soils in regions like El Oued), and extraction methodologies (Salhi et al., 2024). For preservation, the polyunsaturated fatty acid profile, implied by total lipid content, may influence membrane fluidity and cold shock resistance in preserved cells.
[P0080 | Normal] 4.2.3.4 Protein Content
[P0081 | Normal] With a protein level of 37.94 ± 1.10%, the sample showed notable deviation from earlier research. This concentration exceeded values reported by Al-Samarai et al. (2016) (19.45%), Hassan (2011) (31.11%), and ranges in some cultivars (e.g., 19.45–27.24%), while closely approaching or matching high-end values from Egyptian/processed samples such as El-Kholy et al. (2019) (36.28 ± 0.57%) and compilations up to 39.8% (Salhi et al., 2024). Our findings also exceeded typical lower-end reports. This exceptionally high protein content is significant for preservation applications.
[P0082 | Normal] Proteins function as membrane stabilisers, cryoprotectants, and energy substrates during semen storage. Date palm pollen (DPP)'s protein concentration (typically ranging from approximately 19–39%, with values often reported around 30–36%) substantially exceeds that of common alternative or comparative protein sources such as egg yolk (~15.9–16.4% protein in raw form, a staple in animal-based extenders for its lipoprotein protection), soybean (soy protein ~35–40% in isolates/concentrates used in plant-based lecithin extenders, though whole soy is lower), and bovine serum albumin (BSA) (nearly 100% protein, but typically added at low concentrations like 0.2–3% as a membrane stabilizer in extenders), positioning it as a high-value natural protein source for extender formulations. The variability in protein content across studies likely stems from pollen maturity at harvest, varietal differences (e.g., cultivar genetics in arid Algerian oases), and post-harvest processing methods (drying extent, extraction), factors requiring standardisation for reproducible outcomes (Salhi et al., 2024).
[P0083 | Normal] 4.2.3.5 Carbohydrate Content
[P0084 | Normal] The carbohydrate level of 30.12 ± 1.0% exceeded values reported by Hassan (2011) (13.41%), El-Kholy et al. (2019) (17.14%), Al-Anber (2017) (≈18.76%), and Al-Samarai et al. (2016) (26.25 ± 0.315 g/100g), aligning closer to higher-end ranges in some processed/regional samples (Salhi et al., 2024). According to El-Kholy et al. (2019) and related studies, DPP offers simple sugars (e.g., glucose, fructose, sucrose) that enhance energy availability, a critical factor for metabolism during storage. The elevated carbohydrate content in our samples suggests high-energy substrate availability that could support viability during preservation. Pollen ripeness and drying techniques significantly affect sugar composition and availability; the substantial carbohydrate levels observed here may reflect optimal harvest timing and gentle processing that preserved simple sugars in the arid El Oued oasis context (Salhi et al., 2024; Sebii et al., 2019)

[P0089 | Normal] 4.2.4.1 Potassium
[P0090 | Normal] Potassium concentration (1140 ± 20.0 mg/100g) was remarkably higher than previous reports in Egyptian and Iraqi date palm pollen, such as El-Kholy et al. (2019) (750 mg/100g) and Al-Samarai et al. (2016) (735 mg/100g, or 7350 mg/100g in some scaled interpretations from mg/g). This finding validates DPP's position as a potassium-rich supplement, with implications for neuromuscular function maintenance in preserved gametes. Potassium plays critical roles in membrane potential regulation, ion balance, and cellular metabolism, functions essential for sperm motility preservation during chilled or cryopreserved storage (El-Kholy et al., 2019; Salhi et al., 2024). Higher potassium levels in extenders can help mitigate depolarisation and support energy-dependent motility mechanisms, enhancing viability in ovine genetic preservation applications.
[P0091 | Normal] 4.2.4.2 Magnesium
[P0092 | Normal] The magnesium concentration (250 ± 4.0 mg/100g) exceeded Al-Samarai et al. (2016) (196 mg/100g, or 19.6 mg/g equivalent) but remained below El-Kholy et al. (2019) (318.7 mg/100g in Egyptian DPP). This variability likely reflects arid soil conditions characteristic of the El Oued region, where magnesium availability may be limited or differentially accumulated compared to other cultivation areas (e.g., due to saline/arid oasis soils and cultivar-specific uptake in Algerian steppe/Saharan contexts) (Benamor et al., 2024; Salhi et al., 2024). Magnesium is vital for ATP metabolism, enzymatic cofactor functions (e.g., in kinase/phosphatase reactions), and ion homeostasis in cells—roles essential for maintaining sperm motility, membrane integrity, and metabolic activity during preservation (El-Kholy et al., 2019; Salhi et al., 2024). In extenders, adequate Mg²⁺ supports energy-dependent flagellar function and mitigates oxidative/ionic stress, enhancing viability in ovine genetic conservation applications
[P0093 | Normal] 4.2.4.3 Calcium
[P0094 | Normal] Calcium concentration (350 ± 5.0 mg/100g) fell between reported levels in date palm pollen, such as Al-Samarai et al. (2016) (108 mg/100g in Iraqi El-Ghannmi Ahmar variety) and El-Kholy et al. (2019) (560 mg/100g in Egyptian DPP). These differences may derive from harvest timing, cultivar-specific calcium accumulation patterns, and regional soil conditions (e.g., calcareous/arid oasis soils in El Oued that influence mineral uptake differently from other cultivation zones) (Benamor et al., 2024; Salhi et al., 2024). Calcium regulation is critical for capacitation and acrosome reaction control—processes that must be carefully managed during preservation to prevent premature activation and maintain sperm fertilising potential. In extenders, balanced Ca²⁺ levels support intracellular signalling and membrane fusion events while minimising spontaneous acrosome loss during storage, enhancing viability and post-thaw functionality in ovine genetic conservation applications (El-Kholy et al., 2019; Salhi et al., 2024).
[P0095 | Normal] 4.2.4.4 Sodium
[P0096 | Normal] Sodium concentration (450 ± 7.0 mg/100g) showed close agreement with Al-Samarai et al. (2016) (434 mg/100g in Iraqi El-Ghannmi Ahmar variety) and aligns with the upper range of reported values for date palm pollen (typically 200–450 mg/100g across Egyptian, Iraqi, and regional cultivars) (Salhi et al., 2024). Despite being less abundant than potassium, sodium is essential for preserving membrane conductivity and electrolyte homeostasis in preservation media. Sodium contributes to the extracellular ionic environment that supports resting membrane potential, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase function, cellular volume regulation, and osmotic equilibrium—functions vital for maintaining sperm motility, preventing depolarisation-induced damage, and mitigating ionic/oxidative stress during chilled or cryopreserved storage (El-Kholy et al., 2019; Salhi et al., 2024). In DPP-based extenders, this balanced sodium level enhances the physiological ionic milieu (complementing high potassium), thereby improving post-thaw sperm viability, kinematic parameters, and fertilizing potential in ovine genetic conservation applications.

[P0138 | Normal] Having established the socioeconomic context for genetic conservation in El Oued (Experiment 1) and characterised the nutritional and phytochemical profile of local date palm pollen (Experiment 2), Experiment 3 addresses the core technical objective of this thesis: evaluating aqueous date palm pollen extract (DPPE) as a cryoprotective agent for post-slaughter ram sperm preservation. [P0139 | Normal] Post-slaughter sperm recovery represents a critical yet underutilised conservation strategy for valuable ovine genetics. However, epididymal spermatozoa are particularly vulnerable to preservation stress: unlike ejaculated sperm, they lack seminal plasma proteins, antioxidants, and protective lipids, making them highly susceptible to cold shock, oxidative damage, dilution effects, and membrane destabilization during chilling or freezing (De Almeida et al., 2024; Fernández‐Juan et al., 2006; Najafi et al., 2023; White & Wales, 1961). This vulnerability constitutes a major technical bottleneck that limits the practical implementation of post-slaughter genetic rescue, especially in resource-limited pastoral systems such as those in El Oued, where commercial extenders and cryobanking infrastructure are largely unavailable.
[P0140 | Normal] The rationale for DPPE application rests on three mechanistic pathways identified in Experiment 2:
[P0141 | Normal] (1) high protein content (37.94%) for membrane stabilisation, osmotic buffering, and surface interaction with lipid bilayers;
[P0142 | Normal] (2) substantial carbohydrate content (30.12%) for metabolic energy supply (simple sugars supporting ATP generation during hypothermic storage);
[P0143 | Normal] and (3) moderate antioxidant capacity (DPPH IC₅₀ 624.25 µg/ml, driven by polyphenols at 23.55 mg GAE/g and flavonoids at 6.94 mg QE/g) for ROS scavenging and mitigation of lipid peroxidation and DNA damage.

Previous studies using date palm pollen extracts in chilled rabbit and caprine semen have shown improved motility and viability through similar protective mechanisms (Laghouati et al., 2021; Ng et al., 2022), providing a strong foundation for its application in ovine epididymal sperm.

These concentrations were selected based on effective ranges reported in analogous studies (Laghouati et al., 2021) and the compositional baseline from Experiment 2, aiming to identify an optimal balance between protective efficacy and avoidance of potential osmotic or cytotoxic effects at higher doses. Outcomes will focus on key functional parameters (motility, viability, membrane integrity, acrosome status, and oxidative stress markers) to determine whether locally sourced DPPE can serve as a practical, low-cost extender for post-slaughter ovine genetic rescue in pastoral settings.

[P0163 | Normal] 4.3.4 Discussion: Mechanisms and Comparative Context
[P0164 | Normal] 4.3.4.1 Mechanistic Interpretation of DPPE Protection
[P0165 | Normal] The core finding, significant mitigation of cold shock-induced damage by DPPE, can be attributed to three synergistic mechanisms derived from the compositional profile established in Experiment 2:
[P0166 | Normal] 1. Antioxidant Defence Against Oxidative Stress
[P0167 | Normal] Immediately after sperm collection, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production rapidly compromises viability, motility, and fertilisation potential due to the abrupt loss of seminal plasma antioxidants and the high metabolic activity of epididymal spermatozoa (Aitken & Drevet, 2020; Gualtieri et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021). During in vitro chilled storage, the complete absence of seminal plasma leaves epididymal sperm particularly exposed to lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, and DNA fragmentation induced by endogenous ROS generation (Fernández‐Juan et al., 2006; Mohammadi et al., 2024).
[P0168 | Normal] DPPE's flavonoid and polyphenol content, including specific compounds such as pyrogallol, ferulic acid, rutin, quercetin, and gallic acid derivatives (El-Seadawy et al., 2023; Salhi et al., 2024), provides direct ROS-scavenging capacity through hydrogen-donation and metal-chelation mechanisms. This antioxidant activity likely contributed to the observed attenuation of motility decline and preservation of plasma membrane integrity in DPPE-supplemented groups, particularly at 80 mg/mL.
[P0169 | Normal] The clear dose-dependent protection observed aligns with antioxidant concentration thresholds identified in other species, where low-to-moderate doses enhance sperm quality while higher doses may shift toward pro-oxidant effects via redox cycling or excessive radical generation (Agarwal & Majzoub, 2017; El-Sisy et al., 2018; Laghouati et al., 2021). In the present study, 80 mg/mL proved optimal for ram epididymal sperm, maintaining significantly higher total and progressive motility as well as membrane integrity at 48 h compared to the control and lower dose (40 mg/mL). This contrasts with findings in rabbit models, where Laghouati et al. (2023) reported pro-oxidant effects and reduced motility at concentrations exceeding 80 mg/mL in aged or stressed sperm, and in stallion semen, where El-Sisy et al. (2018) observed detrimental impacts on motility and acrosome integrity at higher natural extract doses.
[P0170 | Normal] These discrepancies emphasise that the therapeutic window for DPP-based extenders is species-, context-, and even storage-condition-specific. The success of 80 mg/mL in this study, without inducing oxidative stress or compromising sperm function, highlights a critical balance where the antioxidant potency of DPPE's phenolic/flavonoid profile is sufficient to counteract endogenous ROS without triggering pro-oxidant activity. This species-specific dose optimisation is essential for translating natural extracts into practical extenders for post-slaughter genetic rescue in resource-limited pastoral systems.
[P0171 | Normal] 2. Membrane Stabilisation and Osmotic Buffering
[P0172 | Normal] The high protein content (37.94%) and balanced mineral profile (particularly elevated potassium at 1140 mg/100g and sodium at 450 mg/100g) of DPPE likely contribute to membrane stabilization through multiple mechanisms: surface adsorption of proteins onto lipid bilayers (reducing fluidity loss and cold shock damage), osmotic buffering to prevent cellular dehydration or swelling, and maintenance of membrane potential via appropriate K⁺/Na⁺ ratios (essential for ion homeostasis and motility under hypothermic stress). The significant improvement in plasma membrane integrity observed with DPPE80 (63.9 ± 0.8% at 48 h vs. 55.1 ± 1.3% in control) matches or approaches benefits reported with purified antioxidants and commercial protein-based extenders, suggesting DPPE provides equivalent or superior protection at substantially lower cost and logistical burden in remote pastoral settings.
[P0173 | Normal] This membrane stabilisation effect is corroborated by cross-species evidence using date palm pollen or pollen grain (DPPG) supplementation. Ng et al. (2022) demonstrated that 8% DPPG supplementation significantly improved sperm membrane integrity and viability in chilled goat semen compared to controls, attributing the effect to protein-lipid interactions and osmotic support. Similarly, El-Sisy et al. (2018) reported enhanced post-thaw membrane integrity and acrosome status in Arabian stallion spermatozoa with DPPG addition to cryopreservation extenders, linking protection to the pollen's protein and mineral content. These findings align closely with the dose-dependent membrane preservation seen in our ram epididymal sperm model, where higher DPPE concentration (80 mg/mL) consistently outperformed the lower dose and control.
[P0174 | Normal] The positive effects observed in our protein-free saline-based extender could potentially be related to the reported antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties of date palm pollen (Ashour et al., 2023; Bentrad et al., 2017; Daoud et al., 2015), which may reduce secondary oxidative or inflammatory stress during prolonged chilled storage. Phenolic compounds (e.g., pyrogallol, ferulic acid, rutin) in DPPE have demonstrated antibacterial activity against common contaminants and modulation of inflammatory pathways in vitro (Daoud et al., 2015; Salhi et al., 2024). However, bacterial load, inflammatory markers (e.g., cytokines), or direct antimicrobial assays were not assessed in the present study, and therefore, this explanation remains speculative and warrants future investigation.
[P0175 | Normal] Together, these results suggest that DPPE80 acts as a multifunctional natural stabilizer, combining protein-mediated surface protection, ionic/osmotic balance, and secondary antioxidant/antimicrobial support, offering a low-cost, locally accessible alternative to conventional extenders in ovine post-slaughter genetic rescue programs.
[P0176 | Normal] 3. Metabolic Substrate Provision
[P0177 | Normal] The substantial carbohydrate content of DPPE (30.12 ± 1.00%) provides readily available energy substrates, primarily simple sugars such as glucose, fructose, and sucrose, for ATP generation during chilled storage, thereby supporting the metabolic demands of motility maintenance in epididymal spermatozoa. Unlike simple sugar solutions (e.g., glucose-only buffers), DPPE offers a more complex and multifaceted nutritional matrix that better approximates the endogenous energy requirements of sperm metabolism, including glycolytic and oxidative pathways, while simultaneously delivering complementary proteins, minerals, and antioxidants (Laghouati et al., 2021; Salhi et al., 2024). This integrated support likely contributed to the dose-dependent attenuation of motility decline observed, particularly at 80 mg/mL, where total and progressive motility remained significantly higher than in the control at 48 h.
[P0178 | Normal] This finding is consistent with cross-species evidence demonstrating the protective role of date palm pollen (DPP or DPPG) during preservation stress. Asmah et al. (2020) showed that supplementation of extenders with date palm pollen grain significantly improved post-thaw viability and motility in bovine semen, attributing the benefits to the pollen's carbohydrate-derived energy substrates that sustained ATP levels under cryopreservation-induced oxidative and metabolic stress. Similarly, Ng et al. (2022) reported that 8% DPPG supplementation maintained superior sperm quality and motility in chilled caprine semen over 24 h, highlighting the role of pollen carbohydrates in counteracting energy depletion during hypothermic storage. In rabbit models, Laghouati et al. (2021) observed that aqueous DPP extracts (40–80 mg/mL) preserved motility parameters during in vitro incubation, linking the effect to energy provision from pollen sugars alongside antioxidant protection.
[P0179 | Normal] These studies collectively support the hypothesis that DPPE's carbohydrate fraction acts as a natural metabolic buffer, preventing ATP exhaustion and sustaining flagellar function in the absence of seminal plasma. The superior performance of DPPE80 in our study, without evidence of substrate overload or osmotic imbalance, suggests an optimal concentration window where energy support is maximized while minimizing potential metabolic interference. This positions DPPE as a promising, locally sourced alternative to synthetic energy additives in extenders, particularly for post-slaughter ovine genetic rescue in pastoral systems where access to refined substrates is limited.

[P0183 | Normal] However, the optimal concentration is not universal and varies by species, sperm type (epididymal vs. ejaculated), storage method (chilled vs. frozen-thawed), and even extender base. For example, El-Sheshtawy et al. (2016) found that lower DPPG concentrations yielded the highest motility in chilled buffalo sperm, while higher concentrations were superior for frozen-thawed samples. Similarly, Laghouati et al. (2023) reported pro-oxidant effects and reduced motility at concentrations exceeding 80 mg/mL in aged or stressed rabbit sperm, and El-Sisy et al. (2018) noted that higher doses in stallion semen sometimes failed to maintain motility or acrosome integrity, highlighting potential thresholds where excessive phenolic load may trigger redox imbalance. This spectrum of effective concentrations emphasises that dose optimisation is a mandatory step for each new application, particularly when transitioning natural extracts like DPPE from laboratory validation to field use in pastoral ovine systems.
[P0184 | Normal] In our study, the success of 80 mg/mL without inducing oxidative stress or compromising sperm function highlights a critical therapeutic window where the antioxidant and membrane-stabilising properties of DPPE are maximised while minimising pro-oxidant risks. This species-specific balance supports DPPE's potential as a practical, low-cost extender component for post-slaughter ram sperm preservation in resource-limited contexts.

[P0187 | Normal] 4.3.5.3 Practical Significance for Field Application
[P0188 | Normal] The improvement in total motility at 48 hours (67.5 ± 0.9% in DPPE80 vs. 58.3 ± 1.3% in control) is biologically meaningful for field fertility outcomes. This preservation level substantially exceeds the steep decline trajectory documented for undiluted Ouled-Djellal ram sperm in Algeria, where motility often falls below 50% within 24–48 hours under chilled conditions without protective additives (Safsaf et al., 2015). By attenuating this rapid loss, DPPE effectively extends the practical window for using chilled epididymal sperm in artificial insemination or short-distance transport under field conditions—where refrigeration reliability, timing, and access to facilities are frequently limited.
[P0189 | Normal] Unlike purified compounds (e.g., ascorbic acid, glutathione, or single-phenolic agents) that require precise dosing, controlled storage, and often high cost, DPP offers a cost-effective, multi-compound source containing diverse bioactive phytochemicals (polyphenols, flavonoids, proteins, carbohydrates, minerals) that exert synergistic protective effects on sperm motility, viability, membrane integrity, and acrosome integrity. This broad-spectrum, complementary action has been consistently demonstrated across multiple species, including rabbits (Laghouati et al., 2021), goats (Ng et al., 2022), buffalo (El-Sheshtawy et al., 2016), bulls (Asmah et al., 2020), and stallions (El-Sisy et al., 2018), where DPP or DPPG supplementation improved chilled and post-thaw quality parameters beyond controls. The synergy likely arises from simultaneous mechanisms: carbohydrate-derived energy support, protein-mediated membrane stabilization, ionic/osmotic buffering, and ROS scavenging—addressing the multifaceted stresses of epididymal sperm preservation in a single, natural product.
[P0190 | Normal] For the majority of El Oued sheep keepers who are full-time breeders managing small-to-medium flocks (as identified in Experiment 1), this extended 48-hour window provides critical operational flexibility for genetic rescue operations. Keepers in this demographic—precisely those most vulnerable to irreversible genetic loss from unexpected ram mortality due to limited flock size, geographic remoteness, and lack of cryobanking infrastructure—can now implement low-cost, post-slaughter preservation using locally abundant date palm pollen. The simplicity of aqueous extraction (requiring only water, basic mixing, and no advanced equipment), negligible procurement costs, and cultural familiarity with DPP in traditional Algerian Saharan medicine (Selmani et al., 2017) align perfectly with the practical constraints, knowledge systems, and resource limitations of these pastoralists. This approach transforms an underutilised genetic resource into a viable, community-accessible conservation tool, potentially reducing the risk of losing valuable local genotypes (e.g., drought-adapted Ouled Djellal traits) in the face of unpredictable mortality events.
[P0191 | Normal] 4.3.5.4 Comparison with Conventional Extenders
[P0192 | Normal] Conventional extenders for ram sperm cryopreservation or chilled storage typically incorporate egg yolk, skimmed milk, or synthetic cryoprotectants (e.g., glycerol, DMSO, Tris-citrate-based formulations) that provide effective membrane protection and osmotic buffering but are often cost-prohibitive, pose biosecurity risks (e.g., bacterial contamination from egg yolk), and logistically challenging in remote pastoral settings (Anzar et al., 2019; Bustani & Baiee, 2021). In contrast, aqueous date palm pollen extract (DPPE) offers distinct advantages that align closely with the socioeconomic context identified in Experiment 1, limited formal education (38.5% primary or less), small flock sizes, absence of cryobanking infrastructure, and reliance on traditional knowledge and local resources.

[P0194 | Normal] Despite these advantages, the current protein-free saline-based DPPE extender represents a limitation. Commercial extenders typically incorporate egg yolk or milk proteins that provide additional lecithin-based membrane protection and cholesterol supplementation, which are known to reduce cold shock and improve post-thaw survival (Watson, 1995; Layek et al., 2016). The observed improvements in motility and membrane integrity with DPPE80, while significant, may be further enhanced by combining DPPE with low concentrations of these proteins rather than using it as a complete replacement. Future optimization should therefore evaluate synergistic formulations—DPPE supplementation of protein-containing extenders—to achieve additive or greater-than-additive protection while retaining cost and accessibility benefits. This hybrid approach could bridge the gap between natural, local resources and established extender efficacy, making post-slaughter ovine genetic rescue more practical and scalable in semi-arid pastoral systems.
[P0195 | Normal] 4.3.6 Limitations and Future Research Directions
[P0196 | Normal] This experiment provides proof-of-concept for DPPE efficacy but leaves several questions unresolved:
[P0197 | Normal] 1. Biochemical Mechanism Validation
[P0198 | Normal] The study inferred oxidative stress protection from motility and membrane integrity outcomes without direct measurement of oxidative stress biomarkers (malondialdehyde [MDA], total antioxidant capacity [TAC], intracellular ROS). Future research must quantify oxidative damage reduction to confirm the proposed antioxidant mechanism.
[P0199 | Normal] 2. Extraction Method Optimisation
[P0200 | Normal] The aqueous extraction method likely under-recovered lipophilic antioxidant fractions, potentially underestimating total protective capacity. Comparative evaluation of hydro-alcoholic extracts could identify optimal extraction protocols balancing efficacy with field simplicity.
[P0201 | Normal] 3. Fertility Validation
[P0202 | Normal] While motility and membrane integrity are strong predictors of fertility, direct validation through in vitro fertilization or artificial insemination trials is essential before field recommendation. The biological significance of the 9.2% motility improvement must be confirmed through pregnancy rate outcomes.
[P0203 | Normal] 4. Microbial and Safety Assessment
[P0204 | Normal] The antimicrobial properties of DPP (Bentrad et al., 2017; Daoud et al., 2019) were not evaluated in this study. Since extenders were prepared without antibiotics, DPPE may have contributed to microbial load reduction—a hypothesis requiring direct testing. Additionally, potential toxicity at higher concentrations (>80 mg/mL) should be evaluated to define the therapeutic window upper limit.
[P0205 | Normal] 5. Integration with Commercial Extenders
[P0206 | Normal] The optimal application may involve DPPE supplementation of existing commercial extenders rather than standalone use. Future research should evaluate the additive effects of DPPE in Tris-egg yolk or milk-based extenders.
[P0207 | Normal] 4.3.7 Conclusion
[P0208 | Normal] Experiment 3 demonstrates that 80 mg/mL aqueous date palm pollen extract effectively preserves total motility, progressive motility, and plasma membrane integrity of ram epididymal sperm during 48 hours of chilled storage. The dose-dependent protection (DPPE80 > DPPE40 > control) and time-dependent divergence confirm sustained protective effects that extend the practical window for post-slaughter genetic rescue from <24 hours to >48 hours.
[P0209 | Normal] These findings directly address the conservation imperative identified in Experiment 1: the 71% of full-time breeders managing small-to-medium flocks now have access to a technically simple, culturally acceptable, cost-free preservation tool that requires no laboratory infrastructure. The mechanistic interpretation—combining antioxidant defence, membrane stabilisation, and metabolic support—aligns with the compositional profile documented in Experiment 2, validating the integrated research design of this thesis.
[P0210 | Normal] The 80 mg/mL optimal concentration converges with cross-species evidence for epididymal sperm preservation, supporting the broad applicability of DPP-based extenders in ruminant genetic conservation. However, direct oxidative stress biomarker measurement and fertility validation remain essential before field implementation. These findings establish the technical foundation for developing evidence-based, field-deployable protocols for post-slaughter ovine genetic rescue in Algerian pastoral systems and comparable contexts globally.

[P0215 | Normal] 4.4 Experiment 4: Climate Change Perceptions and Fertility Impacts—Linking Environmental Stressors to Conservation Imperative
[P0216 | Normal] 4.4.1 Overview and Rationale
[P0217 | Normal] Having established the socioeconomic context for genetic conservation (Experiment 1), characterised the biochemical potential of date palm pollen (Experiment 2), and validated its efficacy for sperm preservation (Experiment 3), Experiment 4 addresses a critical contextual validation: documenting how climate change threatens the very genetic resources that post-slaughter preservation aims to protect. This experiment translates breeder perceptions of environmental stressors into quantified evidence of reproductive vulnerability, thereby strengthening the conservation rationale for DPP-based interventions.
[P0218 | Normal] Climate change poses escalating threats to livestock production in arid and semi-arid regions, yet field-level evidence from breeder perspectives remains limited (Ben Moula et al., 2024; Van Wettere et al., 2021). Small ruminants, including sheep, represent critical assets for food security and livelihood sustainability in extreme environments, yet they are particularly vulnerable to both direct thermal stress and indirect consequences through deteriorating feed and water resources (Joy et al., 2020; Wodajo et al., 2020). In Algeria's arid pastoral zones, where sheep production constitutes a cornerstone of agricultural activity, recent evaluations reveal significant sustainability challenges amid high climatic fluctuation and increasing environmental pressures (Rjili et al., 2023; Siad et al., 2022).
[P0219 | Normal] This experiment specifically tests whether breeder perceptions of climate-fertility relationships align with established physiological mechanisms, and whether these perceptions vary systematically by socio-demographic or husbandry characteristics. By documenting the types, frequencies, and temporal patterns of perceived climate impacts, this analysis provides the contextual validation needed to justify field-deployable genetic rescue technologies in climate-vulnerable pastoral systems.
[P0220 | Normal] 4.4.2 Data Cleaning and Pre-processing
[P0221 | Normal] 4.4.2.1 Data Integrity Protocol Prior to statistical analysis, the dataset underwent a rigorous cleaning protocol adhering to the "Janitor Phase" of research (Masterclass, Page 26). Incomplete responses were excluded, and categorical variables (e.g., climate impact types, fertility change patterns) were coded for statistical compatibility. Normality of distribution was confirmed using Shapiro-Wilk tests for continuous variables, and homogeneity of variance was verified using Levene's test. No significant outliers were detected using Z-score analysis (|Z| > 3.29). A sensitivity audit confirmed that data cleaning did not alter the directional trends of the findings, ensuring the integrity of subsequent statistical inferences.
[P0222 | Normal] 4.4.2.2 Ethical and Methodological Transparency This research adhered to ethical guidelines for research involving human participants. The Scientific Council of the Faculty of Natural and Life Sciences, University of El Oued, granted ethical approval for this research. Informed verbal consent was obtained from all respondents following explanation of research purposes, voluntary participation, and data confidentiality protections. Participant identities were anonymized through coding systems, with regional identifiers used rather than specific locality names to protect respondent privacy while maintaining analytical utility.
[P0223 | Normal] 4.4.3 Climate Impact Perception Results
[P0224 | Normal] 4.4.3.1 Universal Recognition of Climate-Fertility Relationships Near-universal recognition of perceived climate-fertility relationships was observed: 95.0% of respondents (n = 190) reported personally observing what they attributed to climate-related impacts on sheep reproductive performance, while all 200 respondents (100.0%) strongly agreed that environmental factors influence fertility. This recognition rate significantly exceeds those reported in comparable regional studies, such as 79.19% among Ethiopian livestock farmers (Abazinab et al., 2022) and high but variable awareness levels in Sierra Leonean farming communities (Sesay et al., 2022), suggesting that Algerian arid environments may be experiencing particularly acute climate stressor exposure or that traditional observation systems in these regions are especially attuned to environmental changes affecting flock performance.
[P0225 | Normal] 4.4.3.2 Hierarchy of Perceived Climate Stressors Analysis of specific climate stressor types revealed a clear hierarchy of concerns, with nutritional stress emerging as the predominant challenge (Table 4.10). Impact on forage and feed resource availability was reported most frequently (48.9% of valid responses), followed by hot and dry conditions affecting pasture quality and water availability (30.0%). Cold and harsh winter conditions were reported by 11.6% of respondents, seasonal variation effects by 5.8%, and humidity-related disease incidence by 3.7%.

[P0231 | Normal] 4.4.4 Statistical Analysis of Climate-Fertility Relationships
[P0232 | Normal] 4.4.4.1 Socio-demographic Predictors of Impact Detection Univariate binary logistic regression analysis revealed that pre-existing belief in climate change was the strongest predictor of reported perception of fertility impacts (χ² = 116.169, p < 0.001). Breeders who affirmed that climate change affects fertility had substantially elevated odds of also reporting reproductive impacts compared to those who did not (OR = 708.27, 95% CI: 36.66–13,683.6, η² = 0.38). Note: Effect size (Partial Eta Squared) calculated to determine practical significance alongside statistical significance (Masterclass, Page 29). However, this association must be interpreted with caution: the extremely wide confidence interval reflects severe model instability arising from sparse outcome data (only 15 respondents reported no impacts), and the association embodies a recognised form of circular reasoning—respondents who believe climate change affects fertility are inherently more likely to attribute observed reproductive declines to climate causes.
[P0233 | Normal] Analysis of socio-demographic predictors revealed that traditional background characteristics did not significantly influence the ability to detect climate-related fertility changes. Age group (χ² = 3.304, p = 0.192, η² = 0.02), education level (χ² = 2.516, p = 0.472, η² = 0.01), breeding experience (χ² = 0.127, p = 0.939, η² = 0.001), and primary occupation (χ² = 0.253, p = 0.615, η² = 0.001) showed no statistically significant associations with impact observation. Detection rates were remarkably consistent across experience levels: novice breeders (< 5 years) reported impacts at 93.8%, intermediate breeders (5–15 years) at 92.8%, and veteran breeders (> 15 years) at 91.9%.
[P0234 | Normal] 4.4.4.2 Husbandry System Characteristics and Impact Detection Herd size emerged as the only structural predictor significantly associated with climate impact detection (χ² = 11.293, p = 0.004, η² = 0.06). Medium-sized operations (20–50 heads) demonstrated substantially elevated odds of detecting fertility impacts relative to large herds (> 50 heads; OR = 24.86, 95% CI: 1.34–461.6, η² = 0.12). However, the wide confidence interval indicates low precision of the estimate, suggesting caution in interpretation. Small herds (< 20 heads) showed no significant difference from the reference category (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.30–2.93, η² = 0.002), with 87.0% detection. Large herds exhibited the highest proportion reporting no impacts (12.2%), which may indicate greater buffering capacity against climate variability, although this relationship was not adjusted for potential confounders.
[P0235 | Normal] 4.4.4.3 Regional Consistency in Stressor Reporting Cross-tabulation analysis examined whether the type of climate impact reported varied by geographic region. The distribution of responses shows that forage and feed resource limitations and hot/dry conditions were consistently the most frequently reported stressors across all regions. Although some variation in frequency was observed—for instance, higher counts were recorded in Robbah and Taleb Larbi, while certain impact categories were absent in smaller regions—these differences reflect uneven distribution of responses rather than clear regional patterns.
[P0236 | Normal] Chi-square analysis confirmed that the association between region and climate impact type was not statistically significant (χ²(28) = 30.85, p = 0.324, Cramér's V = 0.196, η² = 0.04), suggesting that no statistically supported association was detected between geographic region and reported climate stressor type. However, 65% of expected cell frequencies were below the recommended threshold of 5, indicating violation of chi-square test assumptions and limiting the reliability of the test results. This reflects the small number of respondents reporting "no climate impact" (n = 10) and sparse regional sub-samples for certain categories.
[P0237 | Normal] 4.4.5 Discussion: Implications for Genetic Conservation
[P0238 | Normal] 4.4.5.1 Perceptual Validation of Climate-Fertility Mechanisms The near-universal recognition of climate-fertility relationships (95%) among El Oued sheep breeders represents one of the highest rates documented in livestock producer surveys globally. This finding aligns with emerging evidence that pastoral traditional ecological knowledge encompasses valuable climate adaptation strategies developed through generations of experience (Mekonnen et al., 2021; Sharifian et al., 2022), yet remains underutilized in formal climate impact assessments.
[P0239 | Normal] The predominance of nutritional stress concerns (48.9%) over direct thermal stress (30.0%) invites reconsideration of exclusively heat-centric narratives dominating climate-livestock research. While physiological studies have extensively documented heat stress mechanisms (Van Wettere et al., 2021; Arero & Ozmen, 2025), surveyed Algerian breeders most frequently identified nutritional stress as their primary perceived fertility challenge. This perceptual prioritization is consistent with emerging recognition that feed scarcity may be an important climate impact pathway for extensive pastoral systems, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions where primary productivity is strongly precipitation-limited and rangeland degradation amplifies inter-annual variability (Ahmad et al., 2024; Bondaruk et al., 2022).
[P0240 | Normal] 4.4.5.2 Triangulation with Physiological Evidence The alignment between breeder perceptions and established physiological mechanisms strengthens the plausibility of participatory approaches as complementary tools for climate impact assessment. The nutritional stress pathway operates through multiple mechanisms affecting fertility: feed restriction during critical reproductive phases, particularly pre-mating flushing and early gestation, compromises ovulation rates, embryo survival, and fetal programming (Yıldırır et al., 2022; Assan et al., 2025). In extensive Algerian systems where supplementation is economically constrained, ewe fertility becomes directly coupled to rangeland productivity, which exhibits high inter-annual variability in Mediterranean climates (El Aayadi et al., 2021; Rjili et al., 2023).
[P0241 | Normal] Similarly, the substantial proportion (40.0%) reporting both hot and cold period fertility declines indicates that climate vulnerability in these systems is not seasonally bounded but represents a continuous challenge requiring year-round adaptive capacity. This pattern may reflect several interacting mechanisms: (1) nutritional stress continuity across seasons as summer drought reduces autumn forage availability and winter cold increases energy requirements; (2) body condition carryover effects where summer nutritional deficits compromise autumn breeding performance; and (3) physiological adaptation costs where chronic thermal stress exposure depletes metabolic reserves needed for reproduction (Joy et al., 2020; Tüfekci & Sejian, 2023).
[P0242 | Normal] 4.4.5.3 Conservation Imperative Reinforcement These findings directly reinforce the conservation imperative established in Experiments 1–3. The universal recognition of climate-fertility relationships, combined with the predominance of small-to-medium flock sizes (79.5% < 50 head) and limited institutional cryobanking infrastructure, creates a critical vulnerability: valuable, locally adapted genotypes are at risk of unrecorded loss due to climate-induced mortality events. Post-slaughter sperm preservation using DPP-based extenders offers a field-deployable mechanism to capture genetic material from valuable rams that die unexpectedly—whether from disease, accidents, or climate-related stress—thereby converting potential genetic loss into conservation opportunity.
[P0243 | Normal] The convergence of date palm cultivation and sheep production in El Oued, established in Experiment 1, is thus particularly advantageous: locally available DPP provides a culturally acceptable, cost-free preservation medium that requires no sophisticated infrastructure, aligning precisely with the resource constraints and adaptation needs documented in this experiment.
[P0244 | Normal] 4.4.5.4 Methodological Reflections on Perception Research The extremely strong association between pre-existing belief in climate change and reported observation (OR = 708.27) requires careful interpretation. This finding reflects an association between two perception variables—belief and reported observation—and must not be interpreted as evidence that climate change causally affects fertility. The association embodies a recognised form of circular reasoning: respondents who believe that climate change affects fertility are inherently more likely to attribute any observed reproductive decline to climate causes (Getson et al., 2022; Reddy et al., 2022). This constitutes a fundamental limitation of cross-sectional perception research.
[P0245 | Normal] Furthermore, the near-universal agreement levels (95–100%) are consistent with confirmation bias and social desirability bias, phenomena well-documented in agricultural climate perception surveys where respondents align responses with perceived interviewer expectations or dominant community narratives (Kaine & Wright, 2024; Kidane et al., 2026). These limitations are acknowledged, and conclusions drawn from this finding are restricted to the observation that climate literacy is associated with heightened self-reported environmental monitoring—a sociological finding of independent interest that does not, by itself, validate the biological reality of the reported impacts.
[P0246 | Normal] 4.4.6 Limitations and Future Research Directions
[P0247 | Normal] This experiment provides valuable perceptual evidence but leaves several questions unresolved, adhering to the Masterclass requirement for honest limitation auditing (Page 35):
[P0248 | Normal] Objective Validation Gap: This study is based entirely on breeder perceptions and contains no objective data—no reproductive records, climatic measurements, pasture productivity indices, or physiological indicators. The study therefore cannot establish whether observed fertility declines actually occurred, whether they are attributable to climate change rather than other factors, or whether the mechanistic pathways identified by breeders reflect biological reality. All findings must be interpreted strictly as perceptual data that generate hypotheses for future confirmatory research.
[P0249 | Normal] Cross-Sectional Design Limitation: The cross-sectional design prevents any inference about causality or temporal trends. The manuscript does not establish temporal precedence between climate variables and fertility outcomes, and all language implying causal directionality has been revised to reflect associative or perceptual framing only. Longitudinal panel designs with repeated surveys, or quasi-experimental approaches exploiting climate anomaly years as natural experiments, would be required to support causal claims.
[P0250 | Normal] Gender Representation Limitation: The exclusively male sample (100%) reflects structural features of the study context but limits generalisability. Women's perspectives on climate-fertility relationships may differ substantially, and future research should employ gender-sensitive recruitment strategies to capture female herders' observations.
[P0251 | Normal] Statistical Power Constraints: The near-universal prevalence of reported impacts (92.5–95%) created a ceiling effect that reduced statistical power to detect associations with socio-demographic variables. The small number of respondents reporting no impacts (n = 15) resulted in wide confidence intervals for several odds ratios. Future research should employ more nuanced attitudinal scales, mixed-methods designs, or Bayesian approaches better suited to low-variance outcome distributions.
[P0252 | Normal] Integration with Objective Metrics: Combining perceptual data with objective meteorological records, body condition scoring, and fertility metrics would strengthen climate attribution claims and improve the robustness of findings in similar near-universal impact contexts.
[P0253 | Normal] 4.4.7 Conclusion
[P0254 | Normal] Experiment 4 documents that a large majority of surveyed Algerian sheep breeders perceive climate change as adversely affecting reproductive performance in their flocks, with forage and feed scarcity most frequently cited as the salient stressor. These perceptions are broadly consistent with established physiological mechanisms, lending them plausibility, but the entirely perception-based, cross-sectional design means that causal attribution to climate change cannot be established from this evidence alone.
[P0255 | Normal] The study makes a contribution as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating investigation that maps the perceptual landscape of climate-fertility concerns among pastoral producers across a large and understudied region. The temporal and stressor patterns documented provide useful preliminary guidance for adaptation programming, particularly regarding feed security and year-round stressor management. The concordance between producer perceptions and known physiological pathways supports the potential value of participatory approaches as complementary, rather than sufficient, tools for climate impact assessment in extensive livestock systems.
[P0256 | Normal] Critically, these findings reinforce the conservation imperative established in Experiments 1–3: valuable, locally adapted ovine genotypes in El Oued are perceived to be at risk from climate-induced reproductive declines, creating urgent need for field-deployable genetic rescue technologies. The DPP-based preservation protocol validated in Experiment 3 offers precisely such a mechanism—culturally acceptable, cost-free, and infrastructure-light—aligning with the resource constraints and adaptation needs documented in this experiment.
[P0257 | Normal] Future research must integrate perceptual data with objective reproductive records, body condition scoring, and climatic and pasture productivity measurements to move from perception to verified impact, and to enable multivariate, adjusted analyses capable of identifying independent predictors of fertility change.
